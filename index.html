01 / About

How I think about
delivery.

“I'd rather spend an extra hour getting requirements right than lose three days fixing a misunderstanding mid-sprint.”

I'm a Technical Project Manager at Wegile, working with software teams and international clients — currently in Brazil and Canada — on projects spanning Property Management, Banking, Commerce and Customer Engagement. That's a lot of context-switching, and it's become the part of the job I enjoy most.

Most of what I do sits between the business conversation and the development ticket. Someone explains what they want; I work out what they actually need, and write it in a way a developer can act on without ten follow-up questions. More often than not, the quality of a sprint is decided before it starts.

I have a Computer Applications background, which means I can follow a technical conversation without getting lost — but I'm not a developer and don't try to be. My job is making sure both sides share the same understanding of what's being built and why.

05 / Core Competencies

How the work
actually gets done.

🔍

Requirements-First Delivery

Clients rarely describe what they need on the first try — usually it's a symptom, or a workaround, or the thing the old system couldn't do. I keep asking until the real requirement surfaces, then write it down clearly enough that a developer doesn't have to guess. That single habit prevents more rework than any process change I've made.

🤝

Cross-functional Communication

Business, engineering and QA read situations differently. Most of my day is spent translating between them — status updates that tell the truth, blockers explained in plain terms, expectations managed before they become disappointments.

🚀

Release & UAT Ownership

I stay involved from development through staging to production, validating against acceptance criteria rather than handing off after the BA phase and disappearing.

⚙️

Workflow Automation

Jira dashboards and JQL views built to answer a specific question, not to look busy. If a workflow needs three clicks to tell you something obvious, it's worth fixing.

🧠

AI-Enabled Documentation

AI tools handle first drafts of specs and status docs reasonably well, which frees up time for the thinking a person still has to do. It's not a substitute for understanding the requirement in the first place — it just clears the mechanical work off the table so there's more room for the part that actually matters.

06 / Projects

Problems I've
worked through.

01

Property Management ERP

A 30+ module ERP covering leasing, maintenance, billing and reporting for a real-estate client, built and extended over ongoing sprints.
Property Management
Business challenge

Each module served a different internal team with its own workflow and expectations, so requirements arrived in inconsistent shapes and scope drifted easily between sprints.

Solution

Structured intake around consistent user stories and acceptance criteria per module, with change requests tracked explicitly so the backlog always reflected what was actually agreed.

Outcome

Delivery stayed predictable across a large surface area, without losing track of which module owed what to which stakeholder.

07 / What This Taught Me

Patterns I keep
running into.

01
Scope discipline scales with module count

Working through 30+ modules on one ERP forces a certain discipline — you either learn to track scope carefully or you lose the bigger picture entirely.

02
Distance rewards precision

Different time zones mean you can't always pick up the phone to clarify something, so writing has to carry more weight than it would in person. That constraint made me a more careful communicator than I would have become otherwise.

03
Every domain defines "done" differently

Moving between Property Management, Banking, Commerce and Customer Engagement means unlearning assumptions almost as often as learning new ones — so now I go into any new domain assuming I'll get something wrong early on.

04
The smoothest projects were decided early

The projects that went smoothest were never the ones with the smartest developers or the tightest deadlines — they were the ones where requirements were done properly upfront.

05
Most of the job lives in one gap

A stakeholder's description and a developer's implementation rarely line up on the first pass. Most of my day is spent in that gap, checking both sides mean the same thing before code gets written.

06
AI helps with the mechanical part, not the thinking

AI tools handle first drafts of documentation well, which frees up time for the thinking a person still has to do — it's not a substitute for understanding the requirement in the first place.
